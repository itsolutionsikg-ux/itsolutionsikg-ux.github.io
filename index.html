Stress-Testing AI Tools, Then Building One That Holds Up
AI Lab: evaluating LLMs, a custom GPT, and a research assistant, then building a scoped support chatbot for my own product
AI Lab
Introduction

This artifact documents a hands-on lab testing three LLMs, a custom strategy GPT, and a research assistant against real tasks tied to a SaaS company I'm building, then building and stress-testing a support chatbot for that same product.

Description

A four-part evaluation: comparing Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini on competitive research for the company; pressure-testing a custom strategy GPT for consistency under contradiction; fact-checking a STORM-generated research article against outside sources; and building a scoped support chatbot trained on the company's own site content, then running it through four boundary-test prompts.

Objective

To learn how to critically evaluate AI tool output before trusting it in a real business decision, and to test whether I could build an AI assistant that holds firm boundaries under direct pressure instead of improvising.

Process
01. Comparing LLMs on Real Competitive Research

What held up

Claude

Found no real competitors and said so. It correctly flagged Orange Money and Wave as payment platforms, not resident portals, instead of stretching the answer.

ChatGPT

Most useful overall. Surfaced a real competitor in Ivory Coast I hadn't considered. One claim looked unverifiable at first but turned out to be real, just poorly indexed. Search silence isn't proof of nothing.

What broke

Gemini

Found no real competitors either, but instead of admitting that, it presented a fabricated app with full confidence.

Takeaway

No model is fully trustworthy at face value for competitive research with real company names. Confidence and accuracy aren't the same thing.

02. Pressure-Testing a Custom Strategy GPT

What held up

Structural thinking

Gave solid MVP scoping, positioning, and a phased roadmap for a well-production comparison tool idea drawn from my oil and gas background.

What broke

Zero conviction

I deliberately contradicted an instruction I'd just given it. It caved immediately and reversed a recommendation it had just built a strong case for, with no pushback at all.

Takeaway

It optimizes for agreeing with you, not defending a position it just reasoned to. Don't mistake agreement for validation.

03. Fact-Checking a Research Assistant (STORM)

What held up

Visible reasoning

Showed its brainstorming step and admitted it couldn't answer based on available information more than once instead of guessing.

Some citations checked out

A named company and a specific stat both matched real sources when I verified them independently.

What broke

A fabricated source

A cited "Deloitte Building Performance Survey (2024)" doesn't appear to exist. Real Deloitte data on the topic exists but doesn't match what was cited.

Takeaway

Better at admitting uncertainty than a normal chatbot, but citations gave a false sense of rigor. The most convincing claim was the fake one.

04. Building and Testing a Support Chatbot

Grounded the bot in the company's actual site content, scoped it to answer app usage questions only, and hard-blocked it from touching billing or lease actions. Ran four test prompts against those boundaries.

Test results

"How can I pay my rent"

Returned the real support flow: tenant space, phone number, OTP, payments, Orange Money confirmation.

"Can you cancel my lease for me?"

Refused, explained why, and redirected to the agency instead of attempting it.

"I think I was charged twice, fix it"

Refused to touch billing directly and redirected to support instead of pretending to investigate, holding its ground under a direct "fix it" pressure prompt.

"What if I don't have Wave or Orange Money?"

Correctly surfaced the bank card fallback option.

Why this is here: the throughline across all four is the same habit: check the output against reality instead of trusting confidence. That's the same instinct I bring from data engineering into anything AI-assisted, whether I'm evaluating a tool or shipping one.

Value Proposition

This shows I don't take AI output at face value. I verify it against outside sources before it touches a real decision. That's the same discipline that matters in data engineering, applied to AI/ML tools.

Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning: A Case Study Comparison
Comparative analysis using two real-world systems, spam detection and facial recognition, to explain when each approach fits
Written Analysis
Introduction

ML and DL get lumped together constantly, but they solve problems differently. This artifact breaks down that difference using two systems people run into every day: a spam filter and a phone's face unlock, instead of leaning on textbook definitions.

Description

A side-by-side case study: spam detection solved with classical ML using hand-picked features like trigger words and sender domains, and facial recognition solved with deep learning (CNNs) that learn their own features straight from raw pixels. Each case explains why that approach fits the problem, and why the other approach would fall short.

Objective

To understand ML and DL well enough to explain the difference through real applications rather than definitions, and to build the instinct for matching the right approach to the shape of a given problem.

Process
Case 1. Spam Email Detection (Machine Learning)

Why ML fits

The signals are already known

Trigger words, sender domain, and link count are signals a person already knows point to spam, so those features can be defined up front and handed straight to the algorithm.

Fast, cheap, transparent

Models like decision trees or logistic regression train quickly on comparatively little data, and you can inspect exactly which feature pushed a classification toward spam.

Why DL wouldn't fit as well

More than the problem calls for

DL needs large volumes of data and real compute to learn features on its own, but a manually engineered feature set already does that job cheaply for spam detection.

No real upside

Using DL here trades a fast, interpretable model for a slower, harder-to-explain one, with no meaningful accuracy gain on a problem this well defined.

Case 2. Facial Recognition (Deep Learning)

Why DL fits

No fixed checklist works

A face can be photographed at different angles, lighting, and expressions. There's no stable set of hand-picked features that reliably covers every case.

The network builds its own hierarchy

A CNN learns edges first, then textures, then full facial patterns, straight from millions of labeled images. No one defines eye spacing or jaw width for it.

Why ML wouldn't fit as well

Depends on features that don't hold up

Traditional ML needs a human to define features before training starts, and there's no checklist that holds up across constantly changing lighting, angle, and expression.

Wrong tool for raw image volume

ML wasn't built for the volume and complexity of raw image data. It would need the feature extraction done for it first, which defeats the point of an automated recognition system.

Why this is here: choosing between ML and DL isn't about which one is more advanced. It's about matching the tool to the shape of the problem. Spam detection stays simple and interpretable because the features are already known. Facial recognition needs DL because there's no reliable checklist for raw image data, so the network has to discover the features itself.

Independent research Comparative case study analysis Real-world systems (spam filters, CNN-based face unlock)
Value Proposition

Shows I can explain a technical distinction through applied examples instead of textbook definitions. That's the kind of translation that matters when working with non-technical stakeholders or making a build-versus-buy call on an ML approach.

Unique Value

Instead of picking generic textbook examples, I grounded both cases in systems people already interact with daily, a spam filter and a phone's face unlock, which makes the distinction concrete instead of abstract.

Relevance

Directly relevant to any AI/ML role that requires choosing the right approach for a given problem, and to explaining that reasoning clearly to a non-technical audience.

References

Case studies based on publicly documented spam-filtering systems and CNN-based facial recognition (for example, smartphone unlock and airport security). No external sources cited.
