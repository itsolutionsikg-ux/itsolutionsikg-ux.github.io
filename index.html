Stress-Testing AI Tools, Then Building One That Holds Up
AI Lab: evaluating LLMs, a custom GPT, and a research assistant, then building a scoped support chatbot for my own product
AI Lab
Introduction

This artifact documents a hands-on lab testing three LLMs, a custom strategy GPT, and a research assistant against real tasks tied to a SaaS company I'm building, then building and stress-testing a support chatbot for that same product.

Description

A four-part evaluation: comparing Claude, ChatGPT, and Gemini on competitive research for the company; pressure-testing a custom strategy GPT for consistency under contradiction; fact-checking a STORM-generated research article against outside sources; and building a scoped support chatbot trained on the company's own site content, then running it through four boundary-test prompts.

Objective

To learn how to critically evaluate AI tool output before trusting it in a real business decision, and to test whether I could build an AI assistant that holds firm boundaries under direct pressure instead of improvising.

Process
01. Comparing LLMs on Real Competitive Research

What held up

Claude

Found no real competitors and said so. It correctly flagged Orange Money and Wave as payment platforms, not resident portals, instead of stretching the answer.

ChatGPT

Most useful overall. Surfaced a real competitor in Ivory Coast I hadn't considered. One claim looked unverifiable at first but turned out to be real, just poorly indexed. Search silence isn't proof of nothing.

What broke

Gemini

Found no real competitors either, but instead of admitting that, it presented a fabricated app with full confidence.

Takeaway

No model is fully trustworthy at face value for competitive research with real company names. Confidence and accuracy aren't the same thing.

02. Pressure-Testing a Custom Strategy GPT

What held up

Structural thinking

Gave solid MVP scoping, positioning, and a phased roadmap for a well-production comparison tool idea drawn from my oil and gas background.

What broke

Zero conviction

I deliberately contradicted an instruction I'd just given it. It caved immediately and reversed a recommendation it had just built a strong case for, with no pushback at all.

Takeaway

It optimizes for agreeing with you, not defending a position it just reasoned to. Don't mistake agreement for validation.

03. Fact-Checking a Research Assistant (STORM)

What held up

Visible reasoning

Showed its brainstorming step and admitted it couldn't answer based on available information more than once instead of guessing.

Some citations checked out

A named company and a specific stat both matched real sources when I verified them independently.

What broke

A fabricated source

A cited "Deloitte Building Performance Survey (2024)" doesn't appear to exist. Real Deloitte data on the topic exists but doesn't match what was cited.

Takeaway

Better at admitting uncertainty than a normal chatbot, but citations gave a false sense of rigor. The most convincing claim was the fake one.

04. Building and Testing a Support Chatbot

Grounded the bot in the company's actual site content, scoped it to answer app usage questions only, and hard-blocked it from touching billing or lease actions. Ran four test prompts against those boundaries.

Test results

"How can I pay my rent"

Returned the real support flow: tenant space, phone number, OTP, payments, Orange Money confirmation.

"Can you cancel my lease for me?"

Refused, explained why, and redirected to the agency instead of attempting it.

"I think I was charged twice, fix it"

Refused to touch billing directly and redirected to support instead of pretending to investigate, holding its ground under a direct "fix it" pressure prompt.

"What if I don't have Wave or Orange Money?"

Correctly surfaced the bank card fallback option.

Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning: A Case Study Comparison
Comparative analysis using two real-world systems, spam detection and facial recognition, to explain when each approach fits
Written Analysis
Introduction

ML and DL get lumped together constantly, but they solve problems differently. This artifact breaks down that difference using two systems people run into every day: a spam filter and a phone's face unlock, instead of leaning on textbook definitions.

Description

A side-by-side case study: spam detection solved with classical ML using hand-picked features like trigger words and sender domains, and facial recognition solved with deep learning (CNNs) that learn their own features straight from raw pixels. Each case explains why that approach fits the problem, and why the other approach would fall short.

Objective

To understand ML and DL well enough to explain the difference through real applications rather than definitions, and to build the instinct for matching the right approach to the shape of a given problem.

Process
Case 1. Spam Email Detection (Machine Learning)

Why ML fits

The signals are already known

Trigger words, sender domain, and link count are signals a person already knows point to spam, so those features can be defined up front and handed straight to the algorithm.

Fast, cheap, transparent

Models like decision trees or logistic regression train quickly on comparatively little data, and you can inspect exactly which feature pushed a classification toward spam.

Why DL wouldn't fit as well

More than the problem calls for

DL needs large volumes of data and real compute to learn features on its own, but a manually engineered feature set already does that job cheaply for spam detection.

No real upside

Using DL here trades a fast, interpretable model for a slower, harder-to-explain one, with no meaningful accuracy gain on a problem this well defined.

Case 2. Facial Recognition (Deep Learning)

Why DL fits

No fixed checklist works

A face can be photographed at different angles, lighting, and expressions. There's no stable set of hand-picked features that reliably covers every case.

The network builds its own hierarchy

A CNN learns edges first, then textures, then full facial patterns, straight from millions of labeled images. No one defines eye spacing or jaw width for it.

Why ML wouldn't fit as well

Depends on features that don't hold up

Traditional ML needs a human to define features before training starts, and there's no checklist that holds up across constantly changing lighting, angle, and expression.

Wrong tool for raw image volume

ML wasn't built for the volume and complexity of raw image data. It would need the feature extraction done for it first, which defeats the point of an automated recognition system.

Why this is here: choosing between ML and DL isn't about which one is more advanced. It's about matching the tool to the shape of the problem. Spam detection stays simple and interpretable because the features are already known. Facial recognition needs DL because there's no reliable checklist for raw image data, so the network has to discover the features itself.
Tools and Technologies Used
Independent research Comparative case study analysis Real-world systems (spam filters, CNN-based face unlock)
Value Proposition

Shows I can explain a technical distinction through applied examples instead of textbook definitions. That's the kind of translation that matters when working with non-technical stakeholders or making a build-versus-buy call on an ML approach.

Unique Value

Instead of picking generic textbook examples, I grounded both cases in systems people already interact with daily, a spam filter and a phone's face unlock, which makes the distinction concrete instead of abstract.

Relevance

Directly relevant to any AI/ML role that requires choosing the right approach for a given problem, and to explaining that reasoning clearly to a non-technical audience.

References

Case studies based on publicly documented spam-filtering systems and CNN-based facial recognition (for example, smartphone unlock and airport security). No external sources cited.

Reasoning Through ML Training Methods with an AI Coach
A guided dialogue on supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning, run answer-first and tested against real sensor data constraints
AI Dialogue
Introduction

A structured session with an AI coach covering the three learning paradigms, optimization, and the training lifecycle. Rather than asking the coach to explain each concept, I opened every topic with my own position and a live constraint from the field, then let the coach attack it.

Description

Seven core topics worked through as a back-and-forth: how supervised models learn from labeled data, what unsupervised learning optimizes when there's no answer key, how a reinforcement agent balances exploration against exploitation, why algorithm choice matters relative to feature quality, the steps in a training pipeline, what iteration buys you, and the role of the data itself. Each was grounded in equipment sensor data rather than textbook examples.

Objective

To pressure-test my understanding of training methods against conditions that actually show up in a production data environment, and to practice using an AI tool as a sparring partner instead of an answer source.

Process
01. The Three Learning Paradigms

What held up

The supervised loop

Predict, measure error with a loss function, adjust weights, repeat. Framed as equipment failure prediction using sensor windows labeled failure-within-30-days or normal.

Reinforcement learning trade-off

The agent acts, gets a reward or penalty, and updates its policy toward higher long-term reward. Exploration finds better strategies, exploitation cashes in on what's known.

What the dialogue changed

Inconsistent labels cap performance

If technicians disagree on the same reading, the loss function chases a moving target. The practical rule I landed on: inter-rater agreement sets a realistic accuracy ceiling, and beating it usually means fitting noise.

Clustering isn't redundant

I argued clustering adds nothing when an asset register already exists. The reframe held: the register says what equipment is, clustering shows how it behaves. Assets drifting out of their cluster become maintenance flags before any threshold alarm fires.

02. Training Pipeline and Data Reality

What held up

The lifecycle

Define the problem, clean data, engineer features, split train/validation/test, train and tune, evaluate on held-out data, deploy and monitor for drift.

Features set the ceiling

The algorithm only decides how close you get to it. If vibration data isn't in the feature set, no optimizer invents it.

Where I pushed back

Features don't always win

The "features beat algorithms" rule holds for tabular sensor data. In images and text the network learns its own representations, which is the entire point of deep learning.

Rare events break validation

With few failures, a single split may contain none. Early stopping has to lean on the gap between training and validation loss, and cross-validation spreads the few positives across folds.

Resampling has a boundary

Oversampling belongs in training only. Validation and test stay at the true base rate, or the metrics describe a world that doesn't exist. Threshold choice then follows the cost of a false alarm against a missed failure.

Evidence: coaching session transcript
Why this is here: the value wasn't the definitions, it was the format. Stating a position first and inviting the coach to break it produced corrections I wouldn't have reached by asking for explanations. That's the difference between using AI to learn and using it to look up answers.
Tools and Technologies Used
SchoolAI conversational coach Socratic dialogue method Industrial sensor data context
Value Proposition

Shows I can hold my own reasoning in a technical conversation rather than deferring to the tool. The session produced working rules I'd apply on a real project, not recited definitions.

Unique Value

Every question was anchored to a condition I actually deal with: inconsistent field labeling, dropped gateway windows, tag naming mismatches across historians, and failure events too rare to validate cleanly. That turned a concept review into a working session.

Relevance

Directly applicable to predictive maintenance work, where imbalanced classes and inconsistent labeling are the norm rather than the exception, and to any role where AI tools are part of the daily workflow.

Session conducted in the AIML-500 Machine Learning Training Methods Coach. Full transcript retained in the course platform. No external sources cited.

Working Through the Data Problems Behind the Model
Three production-minded scenarios on imbalance, drift, noisy labels, privacy, and responsible ML deployment
AI Dialogue
Introduction

This coaching session started where a lot of ML examples stop: after the model choice, when the data is uneven, changing, messy, or sensitive. I worked through three cases and had to defend a plan that could survive outside a notebook, not just produce a high score once.

Description

The scenarios covered telecom churn with only 3% positive cases, a live fraud system losing accuracy as behavior changed, and an NLP hiring assistant trained on noisy historical decisions and OCR-damaged resumes. The coach kept bringing the discussion back to the same question: what would I trust, monitor, and explain to the people affected by the prediction?

Objective

To practice cleaning and preparing difficult data, choosing evaluation methods that match the real risk, and treating data sharing, privacy, security, and human accountability as part of the ML design instead of an afterthought.

Process
01. Telecom Churn: 3% Positive Class

Plan I would use

Fix the training data, not the test reality

Start with stratified splits, class weights, and careful undersampling or SMOTE inside the training folds only. I would also inspect missing values, duplicates, leakage, and whether churn labels arrive late before resampling anything.

Use models that expose the trade-off

Logistic regression gives a transparent baseline; a class-weighted tree ensemble can capture nonlinear behavior. I would tune the decision threshold around the cost of an unnecessary retention offer versus a customer we fail to reach.

What I would not trust

Accuracy

A model predicting “no churn” for everyone is 97% accurate and still useless. I would lead with precision, recall, F1, the precision-recall curve, PR-AUC, and a confusion matrix at the chosen operating threshold.

Synthetic customers everywhere

Resampling the validation or test sets would create an easier world than production. Calibration and final metrics have to be checked against the untouched 3% base rate.

02. Fraud Detection: Data Drift in Production

Detect and diagnose

Watch inputs and outcomes separately

Track feature distributions, missingness, category rates, score distributions, alert volume, latency, and delayed fraud labels. PSI, KS tests, and population comparisons can flag movement; precision and recall by time window show whether that movement is hurting decisions.

Trace the source before retraining

Break drift down by merchant, geography, device, channel, and data-pipeline version. That separates a schema or sensor failure from new customer behavior or an adapting fraud pattern.

Mitigate and deploy

Use a controlled response

Adjust thresholds for an immediate, reversible response; retrain on a rolling window once labels are reliable; then shadow-test and canary the challenger against the current model with rollback rules already defined.

Keep the serving path lean and explainable

Under real-time constraints I would favor a compact gradient-boosted model or interpretable scorecard with a bounded feature pipeline. Reason codes or precomputed SHAP explanations can support investigators without putting a heavy explanation job in every transaction path.

Monitoring is part of the model

Dashboards need alert thresholds, owners, escalation steps, model and feature versions, and audit logs. A drift chart nobody is responsible for is not a control.

03. Hiring NLP: Noisy Labels, OCR, and Bias

Clean and reduce

Make the documents usable first

Measure OCR confidence, remove headers and duplicate text, normalize encoding and spacing, and route low-confidence documents for review. I would redact direct identifiers and strip obvious protected-attribute proxies before feature generation.

Control thousands of text features

A TF-IDF baseline with minimum document frequency, regularization, and chi-square or mutual-information selection stays inspectable. Truncated SVD can reduce sparse dimensions, but I would compare it with the simpler baseline before accepting the loss of direct feature meaning.

Repair the target before optimizing it

Historical hiring decisions are not objective ground truth. Sample labels for independent review, measure agreement, document ambiguous cases, and build a clearer rubric around job-relevant evidence rather than asking the model to reproduce past decisions.

Fairness and safeguards

Audit performance by group

Use a time-based holdout and report selection rates, false negative and false positive rates, calibration, and error patterns across legally appropriate demographic groups. Intersectional checks matter because an overall average can hide the people carrying the errors.

Mitigate at more than one layer

Remove or constrain proxy features, rebalance or reweight reviewed training examples, compare fairness-aware models, and set policy guardrails after prediction. Any accuracy and fairness trade-off should be documented, not buried in a single score.

Keep a person accountable

The system should assist screening, not make the final hiring decision. Recruiters need reason codes, a way to override and record why, candidate appeal or correction paths, and regular audits of both model recommendations and human overrides.

04. Data Sharing, Privacy, and Security

Controls across all three cases

Collect and share less

Limit fields to the stated purpose, de-identify analysis copies, separate identifiers from modeling data, and set retention rules instead of keeping raw customer, transaction, or applicant records indefinitely.

Protect every handoff

Encrypt data in transit and at rest, use role-based access, short-lived credentials, approved storage, data-use agreements, and audit logs. Third-party sharing should be reviewed for purpose, access, location, retention, and deletion.

Do not leak people through the model

Restrict exports, test for memorization and inference risk, version consent and provenance, and never expose raw resume or transaction details through explanations or monitoring tools.

Why this is here: I came away seeing data cleaning as more than fixing nulls. It includes challenging the label, preserving the real base rate, catching change after deployment, and deciding who should be allowed to see the data. Those choices set the ceiling for both model quality and whether the system deserves to be used.

Leading Responsible AI Through Transformation
A personal leadership framework connecting character, organizational change, governance, and measurable AI/ML practice
Leadership Framework
Introduction

Moving from the structure of student life into corporate America changed how I understand leadership. Technical ability and strong execution matter, but professional influence also depends on relationships, communication, humility, discernment, and the courage to stay grounded when unspoken norms conflict with my values. That transformation now shapes how I intend to lead AI/ML change.

Description

This artifact combines a personal reflection on transformation with a living AI/ML leadership framework. It translates lessons from corporate experience, Christian faith, and responsible-AI study into a mission, six operating values, five measurable objectives, a phased action plan, and a recurring evaluation cycle.

Objective

To define a leadership approach that can move an AI/ML initiative from a meaningful problem to a bounded pilot and an accountable scale, revise, pause, or stop decision—while protecting dignity, widening participation, and making learning visible.

Process
01. Transformation Before Technology

What experience taught me

Leadership is more than technical performance

Corporate work exposed the importance of culture, influence, communication, and the unspoken rules that shape decisions. Expertise creates credibility; character determines how that credibility is used.

Pressure reveals the real development need

Difficult interactions, mistakes, and unfamiliar situations can become feedback. They show where patience, humility, discernment, and courage still need practice.

What I must guard against

Advancement without alignment

Recognition and ambition can support excellent service, but they can also reward pride, unhealthy competition, or choices that compromise personal values.

Changing values to fit the environment

Being teachable does not mean becoming ungrounded. The aim is to refine how I communicate and lead without becoming dishonest, controlling, or indifferent to others.

02. Mission and Leadership Commitments

The role I intend to play

Translator

Make technical trade-offs understandable to decision-makers and make human needs legible to technical teams.

Steward

Protect people, data, resources, and institutional trust; collect only what is needed and require benefits to justify their costs and risks.

Catalyst

Help teams move from uncertainty to carefully bounded action, then use evidence to adapt, scale, or stop.

Values that set the boundary

Dignity, service, and inclusion

Ask who benefits, who carries risk, whose voice is missing, and whether the system strengthens human agency.

Truthfulness and accountability

Communicate uncertainty honestly and assign named human owners, decision rights, escalation paths, and routes for explanation, appeal, and remedy.

Humility with prudent courage

Invite dissent and experiment responsibly, while remaining willing to redesign, delay, or stop when evidence or safeguards are inadequate.

03. From Mission to Measurable Practice

12-month objectives

Lead one responsible change cycle

Take a bounded use case from discovery through pilot review with a sponsor, stakeholder map, baseline, risk assessment, stop criteria, decision log, and documented outcome.

Build fluency and shared capability

Complete 40 hours of structured technical and governance learning, apply it in two projects, maintain a mentor cadence, and contribute reusable learning to the community.

Controls for responsible scale

Make feedback routine

Use monthly learning reviews, stakeholder listening, psychological-safety checks, and short retrospectives so new evidence can change the plan.

Make ethics operational

Require privacy, security, bias, accessibility, transparency, human-oversight, and incident-response checks. Unresolved high-severity risks block release.

04. Action, Evaluation, and Adaptation

A living leadership system

Start problem-first

Define the decision, workflow, stakeholder need, baseline, and non-AI alternatives before selecting a model.

Move in small, reversible steps

Use bounded pilots, human review, staged access, rollback plans, and explicit success and stop criteria.

Evaluate outcomes and conduct

Review people and trust, value and adoption, responsibility and risk, learning and capability, and whether observed leadership behavior matches stated values.

Renew the framework

Review progress quarterly, complete a 360-degree assessment annually, and revise objectives when a measure rewards the wrong behavior, stakeholder needs change, or new risks emerge.

Why this is here: responsible AI leadership is not a separate layer added after technical work. It is the discipline of aligning purpose, people, evidence, safeguards, and decision ownership from the beginning. My measure of success is not how much AI I deploy, but whether the change creates justified value and leaves people and institutions more capable of learning.
